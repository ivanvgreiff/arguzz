\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{chap:evaluation}

This chapter asks two questions of the architecture of Chapter \ref{chap:implementation}. First, \textit{how thoroughly does each variant explore the zkVM's constraint system?} — a question of \textbf{coverage}, and our proxy for a variant's \textit{potential} to sit near an under-constraint. Second, \textit{does that exploration actually convert into finding a soundness bug?} — a question we answer with controlled \textbf{bug races} against known under-constraints. We take coverage first, because it both characterises the variants and sets up a concrete, falsifiable prediction that the races then test.

We state the honest framing up front, and return to it throughout. The coverage study is single-guest-leaning (a full multi-guest generalisation is still in progress), the bugs are one \textit{planted} under-constraint and one \textit{real} CVE whose race is still running, and — most importantly — \textbf{exploration is potential, not a result}: broad coverage is evidence that a variant \textit{could} reach an under-constraint, not that it found one. The two halves of the chapter are precisely the bridge between those two notions.


\section{Coverage}
\label{sec:eval-coverage}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{subsec:eval-cov-setup}

We run the four variants of Section \ref{sec:fuzzing-architecture} — \textbf{Arguzz}, \textbf{Arguzz Bandit}, \textbf{A3 Bandit}, and \textbf{A3+Arguzz Bandit} — for a fixed budget of $N=5000$ mutations each, and measure coverage along the two orthogonal axes of the constraint system introduced in Chapter \ref{chap:design}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{local coverage} — the number of distinct normalised local constraint locations (the \texttt{.zir} equality constraints) a variant ever makes fail;
  \item \textbf{global / CGC coverage} — the number of distinct compressed global contexts (the memory- and lookup-argument residues) it ever reaches.
\end{itemize}
We run this matrix across guest programs chosen to stress \textit{different} regions of the machine — a hashing baseline ($g_0$), a system-call / control-heavy guest ($g_1$), a memory-stress guest ($g_2$), and an accelerator guest ($g_3$) — precisely to test whether the coverage profile of each variant is an architectural property or a guest artifact. Coverage curves are cumulative-distinct-by-first-hit over the $5000$ pulls.

Two facts frame everything that follows. (i) This is an \textit{exploration} measurement on \textit{clean} binaries: every accepted mutation in the entire sweep has zero recorded constraint failures, i.e. no variant produced a soundness violation here — bug-finding is the job of Section \ref{sec:eval-race}, not this one. (ii) The trustworthy data, for reasons we are explicit about in Section \ref{subsec:eval-cov-threats}, is the $g_0$ baseline plus all of the Arguzz-surface runs; the per-guest CGC of the A3-surface variants on $g_1$–$g_3$ is affected by a build-mismatch we discuss there, so we lead the global story with $g_0$ and the clean single-guest measurements.

The post-execution (A3) surface dominates \textbf{local} coverage; the during-execution (Arguzz) surface dominates \textbf{global / CGC} coverage. Table \ref{tab:eval-cov} gives the clean numbers.

The result is worth stating carefully because it \textbf{inverts the hypothesis this thesis began with}. We motivated the A3 surface (Chapter \ref{chap:design}) by arguing that editing the witness directly should reach the \textit{global}, witness-internal permutation arguments that execution-time faults do not naturally perturb. The data says the opposite: A3 is the \textit{local} specialist, and \textbf{Arguzz reaches markedly more of the global space}. The inversion is not a confound or a single-variant fluke — the round-robin \textbf{Arguzz} baseline, with no learning at all, already reaches more CGC than \textbf{A3 Bandit} ($565$ vs $449$ at $N{=}10000$). We diagnose exactly why next; the honesty of this correction, and the mechanism behind it, are among the chapter's more useful findings.


\subsection{Why: Fault Timing, the Co-Fire Paradox, and Bypass-vs-Destroy}
\label{subsec:eval-cov-why}

Three observations, in increasing subtlety, fully explain the complementarity.

\paragraph{(1) Fault timing sets the propagation footprint.} An Arguzz fault is injected \textit{during} execution, so the corrupted value flows forward into every subsequent cycle that consumes it; each of those downstream cycles can register a fresh inconsistency in the global memory/lookup arguments. An A3 mutation, by contrast, is a \textit{point edit of a finalised trace cell} — there is no remaining forward execution to carry it, so only that one cell's local row and lookup constraints register. The effect is large and direct: the mean number of distinct global contexts a single mutation touches is about \textbf{3$\times$ higher for Arguzz than for A3} (Table \ref{tab:eval-cofire}). CGC counts the \textit{union} of distinct contexts over all pulls, so a $3\times$ higher per-mutation footprint compounds into the observed global lead.

\paragraph{(2) The co-fire paradox: reliability is not volume.} There is a tempting counter-argument. The A3 surface co-fires a global failure almost \textit{every} time it fires a local one — $P(\text{global}\mid\text{local})\approx 0.89$ for A3 Bandit, essentially $1.0$ for most kinds — so shouldn't A3 accumulate global coverage just as fast? No, and seeing why is the key insight. $P(\text{global}\mid\text{local})$ measures \textit{reliability} (does at least one global context fire?), not \textit{volume} (how many distinct ones?). A3 fires global \textit{reliably but narrowly} (about $3$ distinct contexts per firing); Arguzz fires \textit{slightly less reliably but $\sim$$3\times$ more widely} (about $10$). Because CGC is a count of \textit{distinct} contexts, the width — not the reliability — is what accumulates, and Arguzz wins on width.

\paragraph{(3) Bypass-vs-destroy: \textit{what} is corrupted decides the footprint.} The width gap itself has a clean mechanistic root. Arguzz's effect on the global arguments is sharply \textbf{bimodal}: across the applied Arguzz mutations on $g_1$, $\sim$$32\%$ produce \textit{zero} global failures and $\sim$$53\%$ produce \textit{ten or more} — only $\sim$$15\%$ land in between. The split is by \textit{what the fault corrupts}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bypass} — corrupting a \textit{terminal value} (a loaded byte, a store output, an ALU result) is caught, if at all, by a single \textit{local} per-row constraint and never enters the global arguments $\Rightarrow$ zero global failures.
  \item \textbf{Destroy} — corrupting a piece of machine \textit{state} (a register or memory value) propagates through many downstream cycles into the memory-permutation and cycle-lookup arguments $\Rightarrow$ a flood of distinct global failures.
\end{itemize}
The A3 surface lives almost entirely in the \textit{bypass / local} regime — its point edits behave like terminal-value corruptions — which is exactly why its global footprint is narrow. Arguzz spans both regimes, and it is the \textit{destroy} regime (state corruption propagating forward) that builds its global lead. This is the same fact as (1) and (2), seen at the level of individual mutations.

\medskip
\noindent Together these say the complementarity is \textbf{architectural, not accidental}: it is a direct consequence of \textit{when} each surface injects (during vs.\ after execution) and therefore \textit{how far} a corruption can propagate. A point edit of a finalised cell is a sharp, local probe; a mid-execution fault is a wide, global one.


\subsection{Does the Learning Help? An Equivocal Answer}
\label{subsec:eval-cov-learning}

A central question for the architecture is whether the learning scheduler (cTS) beats feedback-free round-robin — i.e.\ does \textbf{Arguzz Bandit} beat \textbf{Arguzz} on the \textit{same} mutation surface? The raw numbers tempt a "yes": Arguzz Bandit reaches more CGC than Arguzz ($560$ vs $428$ on $g_0$; $598$ vs $440$ on $g_1$). But this apparent win is \textbf{confounded by arm-weighting}: the cTS scheduler samples per \textit{arm}, and the Arguzz arm space is dominated by one high-arm-count kind, so the bandit over-samples it; its coverage profile is sampling-shaped, not capability-shaped. On the normalised-territory gate that controls for this, the learned scheduler only \textbf{ties} round-robin on local locs, and its CGC lead does not survive as a clean capability win. The honest verdict is therefore: \textbf{on this benchmark the learning does not clearly beat round-robin at exploration}. We return to a sharper, less equivocal signal about the scheduler in the bug race (Section \ref{subsec:eval-race-seamb}), where it turns out the scheduler can even \textit{hurt} time-to-find.


\subsection{Secondary Observations}
\label{subsec:eval-cov-secondary}

Three smaller findings sharpen the picture.

\textbf{Local territory is guest-invariant.} The hashing ($g_0$) and control-heavy ($g_1$) guests cover $\sim$$95\%$ of the \textit{same} local constraint locations per variant (differing by $0$–$2$ of $\sim$33–48). The circuit's local-loc set is small and quickly saturated by the runtime plus the A3 surface, so changing the guest does not meaningfully add local territory. The implication is methodological: local coverage is a poor axis for discriminating guests; the discriminating axis is CGC (and, ultimately, candidate distribution).

\textbf{Coverage saturates unevenly.} Local coverage flattens by $\sim$$2000$ pulls; CGC is still climbing at $5000$ (last new context near pull $4873$). A larger budget would add essentially \textit{no} local territory and only a diminishing $\sim$15–25\% more CGC — useful for sizing future campaigns.

\textbf{When learning can even begin depends on the arm space.} The scheduler must finish a cold-start pass before it can exploit. The A3 arm space is small and fully enumerable (kind $\times$ zone), so its cold-start is short and \textit{guest-invariant} (adaptive mode turns on at the same pull on every guest). The Arguzz arm space is large and \textit{discovered progressively} as execution reveals injection sites, so its cold-start is several times longer and \textit{guest-dependent}. A further consequence of the high exploration floor (Chapter \ref{chap:design}) is that even after the adaptive mode turns on, only a minority of pulls actually exploit — exploration dominates the budget by construction. This is the same floor-geometry effect noted earlier, observed here in the wild.


\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{subsec:eval-cov-threats}

We are explicit about three limits, the first of which we found and corrected during the study.

\textbf{A build-mismatch contaminated the A3-surface per-guest CGC (and we retract the original reading).} The multi-guest guest binaries were built from a source tree that was missing the witness-generation handlers for three A3 mutation kinds. On such a binary those kinds silently no-op — the trace is left unchanged, the prover honestly proves the \textit{un-mutated} execution, and the verifier accepts — yet the fuzzer still logs the run as an applied, accepted mutation. This affected all A3-surface jobs (\textbf{A3 Bandit} and \textbf{A3+Arguzz Bandit}) on the new guests, undercounting their CGC and producing spurious "accepts" that we initially mis-read as a guest-dependent effect (now retracted). The Arguzz-surface jobs are unaffected (Arguzz never selects those three kinds). The fix was to restore the missing handlers and re-run the affected matrix; that corrected re-run was dispatched but its numbers are not yet in. Consequently we report the global story on the \textit{clean} data — the $g_0$ baseline (correct binary) and the single-guest checkpoint of Table \ref{tab:eval-cov}, on which the Arguzz-global / A3-local complementarity holds with no asterisk — and treat the contaminated per-guest A3 CGC as provisional. The episode is also a methodological lesson we carry forward: never reuse a coverage baseline across binaries without exercising the injection path, and treat a host that reports an "invalid mutation config" as \textit{not applied}, not as an accept.

\textbf{Single seed, partial guest matrix.} The analysed data is one seed on $g_0$ and $g_1$; $g_2$/$g_3$ and the cross-seed bands are pending. So the strong claim — that the complementarity holds across \textit{all four} guest classes — is supported only for the hashing$\leftrightarrow$control pair (where it is firm: the inversion is not a single-guest artifact). Whether the memory and accelerator guests activate dormant constraint families is plausible but \textbf{not yet shown}.

\textbf{Coverage is potential, not bugs.} The whole sweep produced zero soundness violations by design (clean binaries). A broad coverage profile is the hypothesis that a variant could reach an under-constraint; the next section is where that hypothesis is put to the test.


\section{The Bug Race}
\label{sec:eval-race}

\subsection{From Coverage to Bugs: A Falsifiable Prediction}
\label{subsec:eval-race-setup}

The coverage study makes a sharp prediction. If the two surfaces are genuinely complementary — A3 probing the local/decode region, Arguzz probing the global/value region — then there should exist soundness bugs that \textit{one} surface can reach and the \textit{other} structurally \textit{cannot}. A bug race tests this directly. We take a binary containing a \textit{known} under-constraint (a deliberately holed constraint, or a real CVE), run each variant against it under the oracle of Section \ref{sec:oracle}, and ask: \textit{which variants detect the bug, and how fast?}

The metric is mutations-to-first-detection and the per-seed hit rate over $N=5000$ pulls $\times$ $10$ paired seeds $\times$ $4$ variants. A detection requires the full oracle: the verifier accepts a proof of the mutated trace, the mutation is genuinely bug-exploiting (not an inert accept), and a clean \textit{control} binary rejects the identical mutation at the constraint that was holed. Crucially, the harness control-checks \textit{every} accept, so a variant scoring zero is a \textit{tested} negative, not an untested absence.

We run two races, one per surface, to test \textit{both} directions of the complementarity: a planted decode bug that should favour A3 (complete), and a real value-class CVE that should favour Arguzz (live).


\subsection{The Seam-B Race: A Bug Only the A3 Surface Can Reach}
\label{subsec:eval-race-seamb}

\paragraph{The bug.} We planted an under-constraint by removing the circuit's decode-equality constraints — the only constraints that tie a cycle's \textit{claimed} instruction type (its major/minor opcode selector) to the \textit{fetched} instruction word. The fetched word itself remains bound (it lives in the memory permutation argument), but with the decode constraints gone, nothing forces the claimed type to match it: a prover can assert that a cycle ran instruction $T$ while the word encodes $T'$, and the holed circuit accepts. The hole is honest-preserving (an honest proof still verifies) and decode-scoped (the destination-write integrity is intact), so an exploit is a \textit{result-preserving} type substitution — a genuine but deliberately low-severity under-constraint.

\paragraph{Why only A3 can exploit it.} The exploiting mutation is an edit of the major/minor selector — in our catalog, the post-execution kind \texttt{INSTR\_TYPE\_MOD}. It edits a recorded trace cell that has no live-execution analogue (Section \ref{sec:post-execution}), and it is \textbf{in the A3 catalog but absent from the Arguzz catalog}. Arguzz therefore cannot apply it at all: the bug is simply \textit{off its attack surface}. This is the implementation-level "what Arguzz cannot mutate" argument of Chapter \ref{chap:implementation}, now made into a falsifiable bug.

\paragraph{Results.} The prediction holds cleanly (Table \ref{tab:eval-seamb}). Both A3-surface variants find the bug in \textbf{every} seed; both Arguzz-surface variants find it in \textbf{no} seed at any point in $5000$ pulls — and, as the falsifier confirms, not because the bug is hard to hit but because they never apply the exploiting kind even once across $50{,}000$ mutations.

\paragraph{Why, decomposed.} The detection probability factors cleanly as $P(\text{find}) = P(\text{apply ITM}) \times P(\text{find}\mid\text{ITM})$. For the Arguzz variants the first factor is structurally zero, so no amount of search effort changes the outcome. For the A3 variants the second factor — the rate at which an applied exploiting mutation actually triggers the hole — is a bug-intrinsic $\sim$$7\%$, essentially identical for both ($6.8\%$ vs $7.2\%$). The \textit{only} thing that differs between them is the first factor, the rate at which the scheduler \textit{chooses} the exploiting kind.

\paragraph{A sharp, honest signal about the scheduler.} This is where the bug race says something the coverage study could not. \textbf{A3+Arguzz Bandit} applies the exploiting kind less than half as often as \textbf{A3 Bandit} ($11\%$ vs $24\%$), because its cTS scheduler spreads pulls across the larger, combined arm space and down-weights that one kind. The consequence is that the Hybrid finds the bug \textbf{markedly slower} — median first find $145$ vs $56.5$ pulls, and roughly half as many total finds — even though the bug is equally reachable for it. So on this bug the learning does not help; it \textit{hurts} time-to-find, by allocating budget away from the winning kind. This is a clean, if uncomfortable, demonstration that a coverage-balancing scheduler is not the same as a bug-finding-optimal one: breadth and speed-to-a-specific-bug can trade off.


\subsection{The CVE Race: The Mirror Direction (Live)}
\label{subsec:eval-race-cve}

The Seam-B race shows one direction of the complementarity. The CVE race tests the mirror.

\paragraph{The bug.} The target is a real, since-patched soundness vulnerability (CVE-2025-52484). A RISC-V instruction names its two source operands by \textit{register index} --- the \texttt{rs1} and \texttt{rs2} fields of the $32$-bit instruction word --- and nothing forbids both fields from naming the \textit{same} register, as in $\texttt{remu}\ x_d, x_a, x_a$ (the remainder of a register by itself). In the vulnerable circuit, such an instruction issues \textit{two separate reads of that one register in the same cycle}, and the circuit never constrains the two reads to agree: there is no rule that the second read of a twice-named register must reuse the first read's value, and no rule forbidding two memory transactions to the same address in a single cycle. A malicious prover can therefore feed the two reads \textit{different} values --- read\#1 $= a$, read\#2 $= b$ with $b \neq a$ --- and claim a result equal to $\mathrm{op}(a,b)$. That result satisfies the \textit{local} arithmetic constraint perfectly (the ALU check only verifies that the claimed result equals $\mathrm{op}$ of the two read values); the only fact it contradicts is that one register holds one value --- and that is precisely the binding the vulnerable circuit is missing. So the proof verifies while committing a wrong output. The patch closes the hole by reading the register once and reusing the value when \texttt{rs1 == rs2}, and by forbidding the duplicate same-cycle transaction.

\paragraph{What ``aliasing'' means.} The exploit does not require a hand-written $\texttt{rs1 == rs2}$ program; it can \textit{manufacture} the same-register condition from an ordinary instruction. The \texttt{rs2} field is a five-bit register index. \textit{Aliasing} \texttt{rs2} onto \texttt{rs1} means flipping that field so it names the same register as \texttt{rs1}, turning a two-register read into a doubled single-register read. If the guest is compiled so that its two operands sit in registers one \textit{single bit} apart in their index encoding --- the race guest uses $x_{10}$ and $x_{11}$, which differ only in their low bit --- then one bit-flip of the \texttt{rs2} field performs the alias.

\paragraph{How $\texttt{INSTR\_WORD\_MOD}$ surfaces it.} The mutation that lands the alias is the during-execution instruction-word edit $\texttt{INSTR\_WORD\_MOD}$: it flips a bit of the fetched instruction word, after which the emulator re-decodes and re-executes the \textit{mutated} instruction. When the flip aliases \texttt{rs2} onto \texttt{rs1}, the emulator reads one register for both operands and \textit{recomputes the result coherently} --- e.g.\ an honest $\texttt{remu}$ of $7$ by $5$ (which is $2$) becomes $\texttt{remu}$ of $7$ by $7$ (which is $0$). Because the emulator recomputed, every present local constraint still holds: the result genuinely matches the now-doubled read. The decode constraints do not catch it either, because they bind only the opcode and function-select fields (\texttt{opcode}/\texttt{func3}/\texttt{func7}) of the fetched word, not its register-index fields --- so a clean \texttt{rs2}-field alias passes decode untouched. The \textit{only} violated constraint is the same-cycle read binding the vulnerable circuit lacks; the proof verifies and commits $0$ where the honest program commits $2$. That is an accept-of-wrong. (This is why it is specifically the targeted register-field alias that triggers the bug: a random word flip usually lands on an opcode field and is rejected by the decode constraints, so Arguzz reaches the hole only on the rare flip that cleanly aliases \texttt{rs2}.)

\paragraph{Why this favours Arguzz, not A3.} The exploit needs the diverged read \textit{and} the recomputed result to move together --- a coherent pair. A during-execution edit produces that pair for free, because the emulator recomputes everything downstream of the flip. An A3 \textit{post-execution} single-cell edit cannot: it can rewrite the recorded instruction word (or a single read cell) but does \textit{not} re-run the instruction, so the dependent result cell stays stale, the ordinary local constraint sees the mismatch, and the proof is rejected. The prediction is therefore the exact \textbf{mirror} of Seam-B: the Arguzz-surface variants (and the Hybrid, which carries that surface) should reach this bug, while the A3-only variant should not.

\paragraph{Status and preliminary signal.} This race is \textbf{live} — $40$ jobs ($4$ variants $\times$ $10$ seeds $\times$ $N{=}5000$) against the vulnerable binary, dispatched with a roughly day-long runtime. At the time of writing the results are incomplete and \textbf{no detection has yet been confirmed} (the strong application-level oracle, which requires a verified proof committing a \textit{wrong} output, has not finished its classification pass). The preliminary, unconfirmed signal is nonetheless consistent with the prediction: among the partial data, accepted mutations on the vulnerable instructions appear \textit{only} in the pure-Arguzz variant and in none of the A3-surface runs. We present this directionally and will report confirmed per-variant detection rates once the race completes. One honest qualification is already clear: unmodified Arguzz reaches this bug only when the guest places the two source registers close enough in their bit encoding that a random instruction-word flip can alias them; the rate falls off sharply otherwise — a property of Arguzz's untargeted bit-flipping, not of the surface in principle.


\subsection{Synthesis: Complementary Surfaces, an Equivocal Learner}
\label{subsec:eval-race-synthesis}

The two races, with the coverage study, assemble into one consistent picture (Table \ref{tab:eval-complementarity}).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{l c c}
 & \textbf{Seam-B (decode)} & \textbf{CVE (value/register)} \\
\hline
A3 Bandit         & \textbf{finds} (10/10) & predicted miss \\
Arguzz / Arguzz Bandit & structurally cannot & \textbf{predicted find} (live) \\
A3+Arguzz Bandit  & \textbf{finds} (slower) & \textbf{predicted find} (live) \\
\end{tabular}
\caption{The bidirectional complementarity. Each single-surface variant reaches a bug the other surface structurally cannot; only A3+Arguzz Bandit — which carries both surfaces — is positioned to reach both. (Seam-B complete; CVE live.)}
\label{tab:eval-complementarity}
\end{table}

The thesis the evaluation supports is therefore a \textit{complementarity} thesis, and a deliberately modest one. The two surfaces are not competitors but specialists: A3 explores the local/decode region and reaches an under-constraint there that Arguzz cannot, while Arguzz explores the global/value region and reaches an under-constraint there that A3 cannot. The honest correction to our original hypothesis — that A3 would be the \textit{global} surface — makes the complementarity sharper, not weaker: it locates each surface precisely. The practical consequence is that the \textbf{A3+Arguzz Bandit}, the only variant carrying both surfaces, is the only one positioned to reach \textit{both} classes of bug — which is the architectural case for the hybrid.

The learning, by contrast, is the chapter's \textit{equivocal} contribution. On coverage it does not cleanly beat round-robin (the apparent win is an arm-weighting artifact), and on the one bug where we can measure time-to-find, the coverage-balancing scheduler actually \textit{slows} discovery by spreading effort off the winning kind. We do not over-claim it. The clearest, most defensible result of this work is the \textbf{surface complementarity} — demonstrated in coverage and confirmed, in at least one direction, as a concrete bug a whole class of prior-art mutations structurally cannot find.

We close with the caveats that bound these claims: the coverage generalisation across all guest classes is still in progress; the decode bug is \textit{planted} (a controlled reachability demonstration, not a discovery) and low-severity by construction; the CVE race — the discovery-grade test — is not yet finished; and every result here is single-zkVM (RISC~Zero). The architecture's value is established as a \textit{capability} (it reaches territory and a bug that prior-art does not); establishing it as a \textit{discovery engine} on unknown bugs across multiple zkVMs is the work that follows.
